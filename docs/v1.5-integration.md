## The five questions

### 1. How much of the length growth comes from the main text versus appendices?

Essentially all of it comes from appendices. From ICLR 2017 to 2027:
- Median PDF length went from 13 to 24 pages.
- The main body stayed at 8.5–10 pages and about 4,300–5,100 prose tokens.
- The appendix grew from 1.5 to 12 pages (median 372 → 4,151 tokens).
- Mean prose per paper grew from 5,535 to 9,812 tokens. The appendix accounts for more than all of
  that, because main-body prose fell by 424 tokens.

The growth is broad rather than driven by a few huge papers. The top decile's share of appendix
tokens fell from 41% to 32%, and papers with an appendix at least as long as the main body went from
1.4% to 48.5%. It holds:
- within submission versions;
- within resubmitted papers (main body +24 tokens, appendix +253);
- in NeurIPS, TMLR, COLM and CoRL wherever the appendix is inside the PDF.

All kinds of appendix material grew roughly in proportion: implementation details 19% of the
growth, additional results 17%, proofs 10%, data 8%, prompts and examples 6%. Post-reference text in
ICLR is at least 97% appendix. 2026→2027 is the largest single step (+1,642 median tokens in
submissions), and it coincides with the cohort doubling. (Lane A; reconstructed by Lane D.)

### 2. What do researchers say in disclosures beyond the template?

Disclosures appear in 96.8% of 2027 papers; the median is 78 words. About 58% of their tokens are the
authors' own (19% template, 22% shared "stock" wording), and 27% of disclosures use no template
wording at all. What authors add, by frequency:

| addition | share of papers |
|---|---:|
| human-oversight claims ("all AI-assisted code was reviewed by the authors") | 72% |
| responsibility statements | 87% |
| non-use lists, mostly the policy's Required tasks negated | 42% |
| scope limits ("solely for language polishing") | 15% |
| named tools (ChatGPT/GPT 9.7%, Claude 5.6%, Codex 4.1%) | 14% |

A minority give long, itemised accounts: AI used for hypotheses, proofs, experiment design and
drafting the whole paper, together with verification machinery (numbers regenerated by scripts,
references checked, proofs checked in Lean).

Copying is common, but it is mostly of non-template wording. 4,566 papers sit in near-duplicate
clusters, the largest with 24 papers. Phrases that separate disclaimers from the rest come from the
policy's task list; the rest is dominated by attestations ("take responsibility", "reviewed") rather
than admissions. (Lane B, with Lane D corrections B1–B4 documented.)

### 3. Can the kinds of assistance be reliably distinguished?

Partly. The drafting vs polishing distinction is usable for group-level description, but not for
labelling individual papers with confidence.

On 150 held-out statements, labelled after the classifier was frozen, the negation-aware rule
classifier v2 scores:

| measure | v2 | v1 on the same statements |
|---|---|---|
| drafting precision / recall | 0.86 / 0.86 | |
| polish-only precision / recall | 0.94 / 0.83 | |
| per-category F1 | polish 0.96, coding 0.94, literature 0.92, other 0.88, drafting 0.86 | |
| exact label-set match | 0.67 | 0.57 |

Caveats:
- v2 passes the Gate 3 bar (P ≥ 0.85, R ≥ 0.80) on point estimates only. The lower 95% bounds are
  0.74 and 0.61, and the bar was set after v1 was seen.
- On simple-random items, drafting precision is 0.71–0.73.
- "None" (0.6% of papers) and "ambiguous" cannot be validated.
- All labellers were Claude sessions, so a human spot check is still owed.
  - Coder 1 and coder 2 agreed on 177/180 items in round 1 (κ 0.96–1.0). They behave like two runs
    of one model, so this may overstate reliability.
  - Lane D's cross-context labels matched coder 1 on 27/30 (round 1) and 29/30 (round 2).
  - Against Lane D's labels, v2 matched 19/30 exactly and 26/30 on the drafting / polish-only /
    other grouping.
- The previous keyword classifier (about 50%) and v1 both failed (N13, N15).

### 4. Are any categories associated with body-text differences?

Yes, modestly and robustly, but as a gradient within a field-wide shift.

Compared with polish-only papers (5,354), papers reporting AI drafting help (11,751) are:
- less likely to say "we propose" (40% vs 66%, adjusted d −0.41) or "a novel" (5% vs 12%);
- more likely to say "test whether" (26% vs 18%) and "held fixed" (16% vs 10%);
- lower on the 2023–24 "AI words" (0.14 vs 0.29 per 10k tokens);
- written with shorter words, more function words, more varied vocabulary and fewer repeated
  bundles.

These differences:
- hold within topic and length strata and against shuffled labels;
- hold with formulaic disclosures removed, with a cluster bootstrap, on the subset where surface
  wording agrees with the classifier, and under label-error correction (larger effects);
- shrink by about half when non-writing AI use is held constant (drafting vs polish-plus).

Null or weak results:
- "results support" shows no difference;
- disclosure length and specificity barely relate to body style (|ρ| ≤ 0.12);
- disclosures share vocabulary with their own body beyond topic-matched controls (+0.057 of types),
  but the stopword-style fingerprint is about 0.01.

The 2027 break itself is present in every group, including declared non-users. (Lane C; Lane D L1.)

### 5. What is the most surprising result, and what is its strongest alternative explanation?

**Most surprising.** The 2027 register break reaches papers whose authors declare *no* AI use. In the
253 papers with an explicit non-use disclosure:
- "a novel" falls from 54% (all of ICLR 2026) to 22%;
- "held fixed" rises from 0.8% to 9.5%;
- "we propose" falls from 80% to 66%.

These rates come from Lane D's recount. The non-use group is small and its labels are unvalidated,
but the polish-only group (5,354 papers) shows the same shift: 12% "a novel" and 9.7% "held fixed".
The break is therefore field-wide, not a signature of disclosed AI drafting. Within it, papers
reporting drafting help sit furthest along: they use fewer novelty formulas and fewer of the
2023–24 "AI words". That gradient is less surprising than it first looks, because the AI-word list
describes 2023–24 models, and current models may write in the 2027 register (Lane D L2).

**Strongest alternative: the 2027 cohort doubled** (42k submissions, about twice 2026; `docs/findings.md` §3).
A new population of submitters could carry a different register whatever their AI use, and the
same doubling coincides with the largest appendix step in Lane A.

**Other alternatives:**
- undisclosed AI use among declared non-users and polish-only authors;
- field-wide writing norms or venue guidance;
- for the gradient between groups, *who* writes polish-only disclosures (second-language authors
  using formulaic academic English) and *when* (resubmissions lean polish-only, but only
  anecdotally: 11/48 polish-only, 7/48 drafting).

**Tests that would decide between these:**
- the same comparisons on camera-ready versions, which carry affiliations;
- first-time vs returning submitters;
- within-author comparisons;
- a venue whose 2026-written papers are not part of a doubled cohort.

## Cross-lane notes

- **The appendix and the disclosure meet in the 2027 statement sections.**
  - ICLR 2027 asked for the AI-use statement in the main text. Statements account for a quarter to
    a third of the 2026→27 main-body token drop (Lane A).
  - About 4% of disclosures leaked through statement removal v2 into the main-body n-gram tables
    (N14).
  - In 2026, 46% of papers put a statement after the references, and Lane A removed these from its
    appendix measure.
- **The 2027 cohort is unusual twice.** It doubled in size and shows both the largest appendix step
  and the register break. Neither lane can separate a new submitter population from changes in
  writing practice. That is the single biggest open question across lanes.
- **Defects found by review are preserved, not silently fixed:**
  - N14: statement-removal leakage;
  - N15: v1 classifier failure;
  - Lane D B1–B4: extraction overruns, the template definition, polarity misfires, stratified
    sampling;
